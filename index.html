The task: say how wild tomorrow will be

Volatility is how much the price swings in a day. Instead of one number, the model gives a chance for each of four levels.

Wild days cluster: a big swing today makes a big swing tomorrow more likely. A classic model called HAR uses that by averaging the last day, week and month.

We cut history into four levels: calm, normal, wild and extreme. Two qubits have exactly four measurement outcomes, so each outcome is one level and the circuit's output is the forecast.

Four steps from the paper to a fair verdict

Scroll through what we built and how we kept the test honest.

  1. Rebuild the original one-qubit model

    We reproduced the published model and matched its closed-form formula to 5.6e-16. We also found one of its three angles has no effect, and that it sees only yesterday.

  2. Give it memory and two qubits

    The new circuit sees yesterday, the weekly and monthly average and the return. Data is loaded again in every layer, then a CNOT links the qubits.

  3. Add classical twins

    Every quantum model gets classical rivals fed exactly the same inputs: softmax, a small network, HAR and GARCH. Any win must beat them.

  4. Split by time and seal the test

    Train on the past, tune on the middle, test on the newest days. Trained models are hashed before test scoring, and pass rules are written first.

Memory helps, and the numbers say so

Paired differences on the sealed Bitcoin test. An interval that stays on one side of zero is not luck.

  • Adding week and month memory cut log loss by 0.051 (interval −0.071 to −0.030).
  • Training the model to also imitate realistic price histories made forecasts worse.
  • The classical twin with the same inputs still beat the quantum model by 0.051.

Ten coins, one rule written in advance: no group passed

Before testing we required a 5% win over the best classical model, on every coin in a group.

Only AVAX passed 5% alone. Its group (LINK, DOT, AVAX) failed together, and the quantum model was worse on a second score (QLIKE) for all three.

What this does not show

Stated plainly, so the result is not over-read.

  • No real quantum computer was used and no noise was modelled.
  • Later circuit versions reuse test days already inspected, so they are indicative only.
  • Twenty seeds measure training variation, not twenty independent markets.
  • The fresh Bitcoin test period contained no "extreme" days.
